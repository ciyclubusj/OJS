Coral Reefs

What are coral reefs, and why should we protect them?


Coral reefs are homes and hubs for marine life to feed, breed, shelter and live. Like rainforests, they hold a wondrously vast and diverse array of marine species ranging from turtles to sharks, octopuses to mantis shrimps, clownfish to sea urchins, up to 25% of the world's ocean species. Such colourful, lively breeding grounds are also fishing grounds for people, sustaining their lives and livelihoods. They also absorb up to 97% of wave energy, protecting coastlines vulnerable to erosion, flooding, storms etc.

Coral Bleaching is one symptom that corals are dying. Triggered by many factors explained below, corals become stressed, and they expel the colourful algae living in their tissue that produce food they need. Although this process doesn't outright kill them, it makes them incredibly vulnerable to starvation and leaves them weakened. If these corals and their waters are left alone, they'll die.

What causes Coral Bleaching

Water Temperature

As climate change worsens, a rise in water temperature endangers corals and force releases of their colourful algae that produces most of its energy.

Ocean Acidification

As more CO2 travel into the ocean, they break down into hydrogen and bond with and decreases carbonate, an essential building block for coral skeleton and shell.

Severe Weather Events

Climate patterns like El Nino can push warm waters toward coral reefs, further increasing water temperatures, causing coral bleaching.

Water Quality & Predators

As sediment and nutrients increase in ocean water, the coral reef ecosystem goes haywire and coral predator population grows (COTS), reducing coral population and diversity. Pollutants also harm coral growth and health.

Coastal Development and Pollution

Factories and other human settlements built near the coast send sediment and pollutants into the ocean that alters the water quality.
